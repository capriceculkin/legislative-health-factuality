What this says

English. The vote is the best English method, but only one difference is clear: it gets 6 lines exactly right that spaCy alone gets wrong, and none the other way (p = 0.03). Against Stanza and the tree, the vote finds a few more lines loosely, but the intervals include zero. All three English parsers agree with each other on about 83% of lines, so the vote has three fairly independent readers to choose from.

French. The vote does not beat Stanza alone. Stanza alone gets 11 lines exactly right that the vote gets wrong, against 3 the other way (p = 0.06, interval entirely below zero). The French vote is pulled down by its two weaker voters. spaCy and Stanza agree with each other far more (73%) than either agrees with the tree (59% and 62%), which fits their shared Sequoia training. The vote does keep fewer wrong lines (27 against Stanza's 37).

Tried and not adopted: letting the vote decide which lines to keep but taking Stanza's wording. It looked better on the sample (English 66 exact, French 40), but on the held-out set it lost 3 English exact lines (21 to 18). Since the variant was chosen by looking at the sample, the held-out result is the one that counts, and it says no.

So, the honest answer to "why three parsers": in English the vote is safest and at least as good as any one parser. In French it gives fewer wrong lines but less exact wording than Stanza alone. That is a measured trade-off, not a win, and it is the decision for Thursday: keep the vote in both languages, or use the vote in English and Stanza with the vote as a check in French, which would break the same-method-in-both-languages rule.

spaCy compared with Stanza

Differences and trade-offs?spaCy decides one step at a time and never revisits a step: fast, but an early mistake carries forward. Stanza scores every head for every word at once and picks the best whole tree: slower, and can make crossing arcs. Greedy parsers are known to lose accuracy on long sentences and long arcs (McDonald and Nivre 2007, studied on older parsers, not spaCy itself).McDonald and Nivre 2007
Does one or the other use a transformer?Neither by default. spaCy (lg) uses convolutions; Stanza uses BiLSTMs plus a character language model. The transformers in the pipeline are Berkeley French (XLM-R) and the discourse parser (xlm-roberta-large). So French constituency has a transformer and English constituency does not.config.cfg; resources_1.15.0.json; benepar README
Sequoia?Both spaCy French and Stanza French train on UD French Sequoia. In French those two votes are not independent, which may be why both made the same mistake in sample #74.

RulesThe rules read each parse into lines

Input
One parse. The rules run three times, once per parse, and never see the other two.
Output
A list of lines: subject | verb | object, kind (stated, embedded, reported), negated, hedged, the rule used (how), and the verb's word position.
Files
preds.py (both dependency parses, and English trees), const_fr.py (French trees), lexicon.py (every word list, paired English and French).
What published method is this closest to?ClausIE (Del Corro and Gemulla 2013): clause-by-clause rules over a dependency parse, with the clause type deciding what is stated.
How do you find the main verb?The root of the tree. When the root is a noun or adjective with is / est, the predicate is that copula phrase. Auxiliaries, negation and particles join the verb ("did not pass", "refused to act").
Which words have a predication?A verb or copula phrase attached as root, conj, advcl, acl, acl:relcl (relcl in spaCy English), ccomp, parataxis, pcomp, csubj or xcomp, or any verb that has its own subject. An xcomp with no subject of its own joins its parent's verb chain instead ("refused to act"), unless it hangs on an object ("urge the doctors to act").preds.py is_pred_head()
How does nsubj, obj, verb each come out?Subject: the nsubj (or passive or clausal subject); with none, it is inherited from the verb it is joined to and marked (inherited). Object: the obj; else a complement; else an oblique ("to the moon"). Verb: the head word plus its chain.preds.py subject_of(), object_of(), chain()
Result of the labeling?Stated, embedded or reported, plus the rule that decided it. There are 23 outcomes checked in order, first match wins: inside a quote; said after a colon; according to / selon; cause, purpose or condition phrase; main clause; joined to another clause (takes its label); the speaker's own framing (I think / je pense); what is said; other complement; relative clause; example; means (by / en); describes a noun; adverbial cause, condition or time, purpose, means, or no linking word; xcomp complement; clause as subject; other.preds.py kind_of()
Negated and hedged words?Negated: not, n't, never, no longer, nor, neither, hardly, nobody, nothing, none, no / ne ... pas, jamais, plus, guère, rien, personne, aucun, nul. Hedged: would, could, might, may, 'd, probably, perhaps, maybe, possibly, likely, apparently / the conditional of any verb (except devoir, falloir), pourrait, puisse, peut-être, probablement, sans doute. Not hedges: should, must / devrait, doit.lexicon.py; preds.py negated(), hedged()
What gets thrown out?Questions, sentences under 3 words, and lines with neither a subject nor an object.
Same in English and French?Every word list comes from one paired file: each meaning has an English and a French side, and check_parity() fails if a side is missing. It returns no mismatches today. 16 mismatches were fixed on October 19 (devrait was a hedge in French only; French trees had no "said that" rule; the stop list was missing 34 function words, mostly French), more on October 20.

What does a match really mean?Same polarity; the verbs share a word, or a word's first four letters; the subjects share a content word; the objects share a content word or are both empty. UniRST vs RST?RST is the theory (Mann and Thompson 1988): text is split into minimal units linked by relations, where the nucleus carries the main point and the satellite supports it. UniRST (Chistova 2025, CODI) is the parser that produces those trees.Mann and Thompson 1988; Chistova 2025
Built on what? How was it trained?tchewik/isanlp_rst_v3, version unirst. An xlm-roberta-large encoder; an LSTM-CRF cuts the units; a pointer network builds the tree; a biaffine classifier picks nuclearity and relation. Trained on 18 corpora in 11 languages, with one shared label set and a mask per corpus. English labels from RST-DT (42 in the model's inventory), French from ANNODIS (20).Chistova 2025 Table 1; Hugging Face model card
How accurate is it?Published end to end on the test sets: English RST-DT units 98.1, relations 55.2, full 53.6. French ANNODIS units 91.3, relations 30.6, full 30.3. What published work does

Henderson and Brill (1999) kept a constituent when at least 2 of 3 parsers proposed it and beat every single parser. Zeman and Žabokrtský (2005) voted on each word's head over 7 dependency parsers; weak parsers still helped, and voting needs at least 3. Sagae and Lavie (2006) merged the outputs into one weighted graph and re-parsed it into one tree. Surdeanu and Manning (2010) found simple voting did as well as complex models, and that the diversity of the parsers mattered most.

The gap, and design 3

All four vote on one kind of structure (constituents, or each word's head). This pipeline votes on extracted lines across two formalisms, which none of them do. The defensible version: parsers that all output UD, a vote on each word's head and relation, a re-parse into one tree, then the rules once. That needs a second UD parser for English.
