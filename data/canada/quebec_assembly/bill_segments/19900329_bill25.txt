M. Cameron:
Merci, M. le Président. It is manifestly
		obvious that public concern about both the natural environment and the possible
		hazards represented by various commercial and industrial products and waste has
		reached a higher level than ever before, in Québec as elsewhere. On
		these grounds, the policies of the Environment Ministry must be an important
		priority of the Government, and we hope to be a constructive critic of these
		policies.
Not only the Official Opposition, but the media and the various
		environmental lobby groups are already expressing great alarm over the very
		substantial budgetary reduction recently announced. I am not so convinced that
		a change in budgetary allocation, even one of 50 000 000 $ is going to be that
		significant in providing us with a cleaner and safer environment one way or the
		other. It must firstly be remembered that both the Government and the general
		public should have a very clear idea of just how and why the money is being
		spent. This is less obvious than it sounds. To begin with, it must be
		remembered that the State itself is frequently one of the worst agents of
		pollution of various kinds and governmental agencies are frequently more
		difficult to monitor and control than the now quite nervous world of private
		industry. Furthermore, public concern itself can have an unfortunate, and very
		powerful backfire effect on issues relating to the environment. Every time a
		substance is publicized as being even mildly hazardous or just non-degradable,
		there is a tendency for both government agencies and private enterprises to
		begin a desaster cycle of stockpiling and transportation, or of inefficient
		means of destruction. The consequences, as we have now seen in several cases,
		is an environmental hazard or actual disaster of a kind that might never have
		taken place if no one knew about the stuff in the first place.
It is also important to distinguish between the widespread but rather
		vaguely focused public desire for a better environment - which can be
		manifested in very healthy developments like improvements in behavior of
		individual citizens, greater attention to conservation and careful waste
		disposal and the like - and demands for laws or regulations which can actually
		entrench the kind of backfire effects I have already noted.
For example, a legal assault on almost any means of generating energy
		usually just leads to energy generation taking place somewhere else, often
		producing far worse effects than the original cause of complaint. The major
		effect of the anti-nuclear lobby in the United States was to bring about
		substantial increases in the amount of coal mining and the use of coal-fired
		furnaces, which have already done far more harm to human beings. The banning of
		a chemical product on the grounds that it has some dangerous, but at least
		well-understood properties may eventually lead to its gradual, almost casual
		replacement by a chemical product about which far less is known, and which
		leads to entirely new hazards not yet imagined.
And finally, a despairing rebellion against all such products would lead
		to both an almost totalitarian level of government regulation and a breakdown
		of the industrial economy. And the eager intervention, even at present, of
		federal, provincial and municipal authorities may lead to them stumbling on
		each others' feet.
We believe there are ways of dealing with these problems and will be
		suggesting some to the Government. In the meantime, we suggest they talk to
		competent economists, at least, as often as to ecologists. The latter are more
		sensitive to the actual threats to living things, but are often somewhat
		unworldly about the real effects of government legislation. Merci, M. le
		Président.
