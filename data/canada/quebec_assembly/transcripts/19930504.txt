Augmentation du nombre des prestataires
d'assurance-chômage et d'aide
sociale au Québec
M. Parizeau:
m. le président, il y a déjà
		quelque temps, un an et demi à peu près, le conseil des affaires
		sociales et de la famille au québec avait annoncé qu'il y avait
		probablement au-delà de 20 % des québécois susceptibles de
		travailler et qui étaient en chômage. on a, de l'autre
		côté de la chambre, mis en doute ces chiffres et, comme le conseil
		des affaires sociales et de la famille s'entêtait à
		dénoncer ce québec cassé en deux, ces deux québec
		dans un, bien, on a aboli le conseil des affaires sociales et de la famille.
		comme on l'a soulevé souvent, soit le ministre responsable de la
		main-d'oeuvre et de la sécurité du revenu ou le premier ministre
		lui-même, ce chiffre-là mélangeait des pommes et des
		oranges. eh bien, nous avons démêlé ça, grâce
		aux services du ministère de la main-d'oeuvre et de la
		sécurité du revenu, et il est maintenant possible
		d'établir pour, par exemple, le dernier mois, le mois d'avril, que, au
		québec, des gens sur l'assurance-chômage ou adultes aptes au
		travail et sur l'aide sociale, ils étaient, au total, 828 000 ? 828
		000 dans une population de 7 000 000 d'habitants. ça représente,
		m. le président, au-delà de 22 %: 22,5 % de la main-d'oeuvre
		active dans le sens large du terme, c'est-à-dire tous ceux qui sont
		employés, qui cherchent du travail ou qui sont aptes au travail et sur
		l'aide sociale, près du quart de la population adulte du
		québec.
Puis-je demander au premier ministre, d'abord, s'il est d'accord avec
		cette constatation? Est-ce que, pour une fois, au lieu de nier que c'est
		vraiment la situation, il va reconnaître que plus de 20 %, près du
		quart des adultes aptes au travail au Québec sont, à l'heure
		actuelle, en chômage? (14 h 10)
Le Président:
M. le premier ministre.
Des voix:
Bravo! Bravo! Bravo!
M. Bourassa:
M. le Président, je voudrais mentionner que
		nous avons eu l'occasion, il y a quelques semaines ou quelques mois, de
		discuter de ces ques-
tions-là périodiquement, quand on publie les statistiques.
		Nous devons constater que dans une période de récession ? on
		n'a pas les chiffres comparatifs pour ailleurs ou d'autres pays ? ce n'est
		pas nouveau qu'il y ait une augmentation des travailleurs qui sont en
		chômage et une augmentation des assistés sociaux.
Je suis obligé quand même de mentionner au chef de
		l'Opposition que nous avons posé des gestes très concrets dans le
		domaine de la fiscalité, que les seuils d'imposition sont passés,
		depuis sept ans, de 12 000 $ à 24 000 $ pour les familles
		monoparentales; pour les couples avec deux enfants, de 10 000 $ à 26 000
		$.
Je donne... Parce qu'on dit, finalement, que la pauvreté peut
		atteindre les bas revenus... La parité de l'aide sociale pour les moins
		de 30 ans, l'augmentation du salaire minimum depuis 1986, la mise sur pied du
		programme APTE, etc. Donc, nous faisons face à une situation qui est
		très difficile. Je suis d'accord que nous devons poser tous les gestes
		nécessaires pour réduire la pauvreté et créer des
		emplois. Ce n'est pas nouveau. C'est notre priorité des
		priorités. Je dois constater qu'il y a beaucoup de mots dans la question
		du chef de l'Opposition, mais il n'y a pas la moindre lueur ou le moindre
		indice d'élément de solution.

En question complémentaire.

		l'obtention d'emploi pour plusieurs dans notre société, est au

		? que, ce matin, le ministre de la Main-d'oeuvre et de la
		Sécurité du revenu, interrogé par la députée
		de Hochelaga-Maisonneuve, a reconnu que, sur les 275 000 000 $ annoncés
		depuis trois mois ? trois ans, pardon ? en crédits aux

		ministre est au fait de ça?
Le Président:
M. le premier ministre.
M. Bourassa:
Le chef de l'Opposition m'a parlé de
		pauvreté dans sa première question. Je lui ai dit qu'on avait
		posé des gestes, mais, pour ceux qui ne travaillent pas, j'ai
		mentionné d'autres éléments comme le programme APTE. Mais
		on ne peut pas distinguer la pauvreté simplement avec ceux qui n'ont pas
		travaillé ou n'ont pas à travailler. Il faut tenir compte qu'il y
		a certains niveaux de revenus qui se trouvent à impliquer une
		augmentation de la pauvreté et nous avons posé des gestes
		à cet égard. Même chose pour le salaire minimum.
Pour ce qui a trait à la main-d'oeuvre, le ministre a
		déjà répondu à cet égard-là, à
		plusieurs reprises, que, lorsque les programmes sont annoncés, le
		démarrage se fait forcément plus lentement. Et nous pouvons
		donner, à cet égard-là, des chiffres de croissance. Il en
		a donné quelques-uns, il y a plusieurs semaines, et qui se trouvent
		à mettre en relief les progrès des politiques gouvernementales en
		matière de main-d'oeuvre. Nous souhaitons vivement nous entendre avec le
		gouvernement fédéral à cet égard-là; je le
		disais il y a une dizaine de jours. C'est vrai que les Québécois,
		sur le conseil de nos amis d'en face, ont voté contre l'accord de
		Charlottetown. Vous vous souvenez, vous avez pris position contre l'accord de
		Charlottetown, oui, protégeant l'héritage de Pierre Elliot
		Trudeau à cet égard-là, oui.
Des voix:
Ha, ha, ha!

le premier ministre.

The question of the liberty of the press has become a very complex one
		at the end of this century. Freedom of the press is not threatened in exactly
		the same way, at the time of 1990's, as it was at several times in this
		century, or in large areas of the world. On the contrary, today we can see, in
		places like the former USSR and the States of Eastern Europe, people beginning
		to enjoy, sometimes with a certain sense of shock, the whole wild variety of
		opinions that a free press can make available to them. We are familiar, as
		well, with a modern kind of media that brings to us events all over the world,
		frequently with attempts at censorship but where even the attempts themselves
		are often defeated by the sophistication of the technology.
We knew we were living in a different kind of world when we heard that
		Mr. Gorbatchev himself, when he was trying to figure out what was going on in
		the Soviet Union, was watching Ted Turner's CNN. We knew that war itself had
		become a different kind of phenomenon, even from the era of Vietnam, when we
		saw the way the war in the Gulf was covered and the way, for that matter, some
		of the horrors that are taking place in the former Yugoslavia are covered
		today.
The problem, I think, that we are faced, sometimes in Government and
		certainly in society, with the situation of the press today, is not one so much
		of censorship by the State or control by the State, as the possibility of a
		kind of organized and uniform opinion in various parts of society that does not
		really require State sanction and where, in fact, it may require the State or
		the exercise of the law to guarantee the preservation of freedom. In other
		words, whereas 200 years ago the great threat to freedom of the press would
		come directly from government, even from, let us say, the American
		revolutionaries: under conditions of difficulty we find someone like John Adams
		producing the Alien and Sedition Acts or Abraham Lincoln feeling that he had to
		suppress freedom of the press in the American Civil War. In other words, we saw
		the definite fear, even in free societies, never mind dictatorships or
		totalitarian States, that the press could undermine the security of the State
		or the safety of the State.
That is seldom the danger today. If we see a danger today, sometimes in
		the press or in the media generally, it is more likely to come from movements
		like political correctness. That is, from something that does not originate
		with government at all, but, instead, is a pervasive social movement of a
		peculiar kind, where the very power of the modern media makes it possible for
		it to spread in places like university campuses or other venues and effectively
		make it impossible to express unpopular views or unpopular opinions. Perhaps
		that is not even the right way to put it. It makes it impossible to express
		popular views and popular opinions if they do not happen to fit a particular
		straitjacket that has been dreamed up by a group of fashionable individuals at
		that moment. I expect that that will become a greater and greater problem and
		one that we will be worried about more in the next century than we will about
		traditional problems of freedom of the press. But we still have to worry about
		freedom of the press, especially the written word, because it is freedom of the
		press that makes it possible for people to see the truth at
some point.
The great philosopher Hannah Arendt once commented about propaganda
		that, if you have lived in a society that is filled with propaganda, you
		realize that its purpose is not to convince you, it is to undermine your
		confidence that the truth can be found out. It is the great advantage of a free
		society, that is, despite all the rubbish we produce, the supermarket tabloids,
		the ghastly and infantile stories, even in our major newspapers at times, that
		the possibility is always there. That is one of the treasures of a free society
		and that is why we must all continue to defend the freedom of the press.
Merci, M. le Président.

M. Neil Cameron
M. Cameron:
Merci, M. le Président.
I do not think I could compete with such eloquence as that shown by the
		member for Drummond-ville or the member for Jonquière. Or, I must say,
		before saying anything else about May Day, that I feel I should extend some
		sympathy to the Minister of Labor because, when it comes to «motions sans
		préavis», when they come from almost any other minister, it is
		usually a moment of relief when they come up with something that is so
		uncontroversial, that our colleagues over there or over here will treat them
		fairly amiably whereas the Minister of Labor usually has to go through just as
		unpleasant time as he does in question period.
I am quite willing to join with other members in saluting the
		Québec worker and extending my thanks for the contribution the
		Québec worker makes to our society. I will do so with the reservation
		that I have made before, that I have never quite got used to May Day, for a
		variety of reasons.
You know, the American comedian Woody Allen once came out with a
		sacrilegious but interesting comment. He said that Easter always baffled him
		because he had a sort of vague impression of a crucified Easter bunny. In the
		process, he was indicating something that happens to dates, the strange way
		their meaning is transformed. All Hallow Even, I suppose, was once the most
		terrifying night of the year, centuries ago, but now it is just Halloween and
		the time of pumpkins and pumpkin pies and little children going out in masks.
		May Day used to be a symbol of fertility. In fact, I think the maypole was
		probably a phallic symbol associated with the whole idea of rebirth and
		reproduction and new generations arising at this time of year. It was not
		especially associated with labour, although it was a sort of peasant holiday
		across Europe.
Then came this famous business about the Hay-market martyrs of 1886, and
		even that is a bit odd when you think back on it. Big Bill Heywood, the man who
		was the main symbol of the Haymarket explosion, was what was called a Wobbly.
		And the workers' movement he represented was neither that of organized labour
		as we generally understand it today or even that of, say, the Marxist
		Communists, but rather of a kind of syndicalism which dreamed of one big union
		that would take in the whole of society. Then the Communists took over the Day
		and for a very long time May 1st, as I think I mentioned once before in this
		Assembly, was not a respectable day to celebrate labour in Western democra-
		7tic countries. It was only so celebrated in Eastern Communist countries where,
		on the whole, really, labour did not have much to celebrate except in rhetoric.
		In September, Labour Day was the traditional one in Western countries. Somehow,
		May Day became respectable, and since it now seems to have become respectable,
		I am quite willing to join in the celebration with everyone else.
If I do so, perhaps I could say something a little different than my two
		previous colleagues on the whole question of how we look at labour in
		Québec today. I think that there can be a certain confusion in what is
		said on this subject, because there are, after all, two very important
		distinctions about labour that are not really made in the resolution or in many
		talks about working people in Québec. The first is, of course, that most
		of the time, when we use this language, we are talking about organized labour,
		unionized labour, which is obviously an important, significant part of the
		working people of Québec, but for a long, long time has not necessarily,
		for example, constituted the group of workers who are most likely to be
		exploited or underpaid or face difficult conditions. On the contrary, in many
		areas, they could be described as a fairly privileged group.
There is another distinction, I think, that is even more important,
		however, that matters for both organized workers and unorganized workers, and
		that is that we do two kinds of work, all of us, no matter what our occupation:
		that kind that we find rewarding and fulfilling because it gives meaning to our
		lives and we use it to support ourselves and our families, and we find it
		interesting and enjoyable, and that part which we regard simply as a miserable
		burden that we have to get through because there is no way to avoid it. (16 h
		10)
The trouble with just saying something like «Let us celebrate the
		workers and the work of Québec» is that it does not distinguish
		between the work you want to do and the work you do not want to do. What I am
		talking about, in practical terms, that concerns this Assembly, for instance,
		is that I remember every now and then I see a story in the press that says such
		and such a kind of factory has closed and has eliminated, therefore, 200 jobs
		or some such number. Well, when I first lived in Québec, I used to work
		in places like that, and I must admit my inclination is to say: Thank heavens
		they finally closed the place! Some of those places that manufactured at the
		lowest level of safety, working conditions, pay, conditions for workers
		generally and so on were awful dumps. They were marginal contributions to the
		economy, and while I did not look forward to the idea of seeing the men and
		women who worked there being thrown into unemployment, if, instead, it meant
		that capital and resources and energies were moving to some other part of the
		Québec economy that could provide them with jobs in the end that would
		be better than the ones they would get there, then I would say:
not every factory closing is a disaster. Not every mine closing is a
		disaster, as any miner would tell you. Not every agricultural labouring job
		disappearance, being replaced by more modern agricultural machinery is a
		tragedy.
On the contrary, it is the changes of that kind that make North American
		society. They sometimes provide us with leisure we do not want, and because we
		guarantee high wages, we nowadays virtually guarantee high unemployment as
		well. And my colleague from Drum-mond is quite correct, as is my colleague from
		Jonquiè-re, that we are going through a technological revolution that
		means that the entire meaning of labour and how it will be used in the next
		century is going to be different.
But what I think we have to remember consistently is that an increase in
		leisure, if it is handled intelligently by Québec society, by
		Québec institutions, by educational institutions, by our social and
		political leaders, is not necessarily something that means the samething as in
		increase in poverty or an increase in social maladjustment. There is a very
		sharp difference between these two things and I wanted to say something about
		it because I think to some extend, if we are going to provide enough work for a
		young people in years of the future, it may have to involve some sort of device
		like job sharing or else those people who are in the higher income brackets
		realizing that they might have to give up just a chunk at what they do, not
		money, not taxes, not income to the State, but their actual occupation just to
		provide a reasonable opportunity for the rising generation, because if they do
		not, our existing system of contracts, of job security, of seniority rules, of
		all the rest of it is so thorough that a very large proportion of the young
		people of Québec will not get a chance, even if the economy is in fact
		doing well. We could see our statistics looking good, we could see growth
		figures, all that sort of things, the deficit being brought under control, all
		of those other kinds of indicators that generally please the economists, and we
		could still see a younger generation moving toward something like despair. So,
		when we do salute the workers of Québec, let us also remember that, in
		the future, they may not work the same way, under the same conditions, with the
		same kind of unions or the same kind of contracts and let us hope some of the
		union leaders remember that this may be necessary too.
Merci, M. le Président.
