M. Cameron:
I think that the law that is being presented by the
		Minister of Labour on Workmen's Compensation is a perfectly reasonable law and
		that, contrary to the view expressed by my distinguished colleague for
		Pointe-aux-Trembles, it is not a bad law. The Minister has launched a rather
		ferocious attack on the previous law as it existed or as it was created by the
		Parti québécois. I think he was a little too hard on them, in the
		sense that the old law was one that perhaps could have been passed by Liberals
		or by péquistes or by anyone else and that none of us really knew a few
		years ago exactly what the implications would be of that law. I also believe
		that in substance, the changes proposed in the law are good changes, valuable
		changes and probably will be essentially satisfactory to the working people of
		Québec, to the unionized organizations and to the general
		population.
I also believe that it is important to say something about this issue
		and this is the reason why I chose to speak on this law, because to maintain a
		successful law on Workmen's Compensation, something that probably many people
		think of as a relatively minor aspect of our politics, is in fact one of the
		most fundamental things we can do in our society, and I believe that it should
		not be underestimated by either members of the Government or by Members of the
		Opposition.
Workmen's Compensation is one of the most brilliant ideas that free
		governments and free societies ever figured out. If it had not been
		successfully established, either there would have been a rotten deal for the
		working person or, at best, there would have been a wonderful deal for the
		lawyers. Everything would have become a matter of litigation and every single
		actual working man or working woman who faced injury or suffering or misery
		would have to take his chances with the lawyers and with the courts. To develop
		a system that was recognized and understood by the general population, by the
		politicians, by the State, by the unions, that could in fact seem equitable and
		fair to all parties concerned, is one of the things that made our society a
		freer, better, safer, more decent society. It is a system that must be
		maintained. In the United States, for example, the Workmen's Compensation
		System is already facing serious danger of collapse, as lawyers use certain
		provisions and certain legal interpretations to force every single case of
		injury into the form of adversarial argument that can produce the most
		spectacular claims for workers.
Individual workers, in the short term, may benefit from such a
		procedure, but, in the long run, that means that insurance claims rise,
		governments get more broke, other funds for other vital services to other
		workers or to the general population are cut, and the whole thing
involves a kind of anarchy, a restoration of something that is almost
		medieval rather than being modern and forward-looking. (22 h 40)
I believe that the Workmen's Compensation model is a very good model,
		and a one that we should think of not only in that case, but about many
		situations in our society. Wherever we can avoid non-adversarial relationships,
		but ones which are still equitable and fair to all the parties concerned, then
		those of us who are responsible as elected Members should advocate those means,
		because wherever we do not, we, instead, give the maximum power, the maximum
		support to the people who like generating hostility, anger, rage and waste. If
		we can maintain enough institutions in our society that are based on
		cooperation, mutual understanding, fair play, voluntary agreement and, in most
		cases, no lawyers even being allowed into the room, we will be a more
		prosperous, more successful, more competitive and happier society. This law is
		therefore a step in the right direction. Toward that end and therefore, we
		support the law. Merci, M. le Président.
